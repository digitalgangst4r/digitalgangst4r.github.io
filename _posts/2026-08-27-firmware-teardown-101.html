PART I

Hardware: getting the bytes off the chip

You can't analyze what you can't extract. This half is the physical craft: a safe bench, the two instruments you touch every session, and every realistic way onto the flash, from clipping an 8 pin chip to faulting a locked CPU into handing you its secrets.

01

Bench, safety & best practices

Before you dump a single byte, set up a bench that won't kill your chips, brick your target, or hurt you. This stuff is boring right up until the moment it saves a board (or a finger), so get it in muscle memory early.

ESD is the zap you never feelbasics

Walking across carpet charges you to a few kilovolts. You won't feel a spark under about 3 kV, but the touchiest parts start dying below 250 V, so you can wound a flash chip and never know until it acts flaky weeks later. A cheap grounded mat plus a wrist strap keeps you and the board at the same potential so nothing ever discharges through the die. And the resistor in that strap is for ESD, not shock protection, so take the strap off before you go near anything mains powered.

A current limited supply is your seatbeltbasics

A bench PSU lets you set a voltage and a max current. Dial the voltage to whatever the board's rail wants (measure it first) and set the current limit just above the expected draw. Now a solder bridge or a backwards chip just trips the limit instead of dumping unlimited amps into a dead short. If the supply slams to the limit and the voltage sags, that's a short, kill it and go find it.

Back up before you write, alwayshabit

The number one way people brick a device is writing before reading. Dump the whole chip, read it twice, and confirm the two hashes match so you know the read itself was clean. That verified image is your undo button. If you can't get two matching reads, fix the clip before you touch the write path, because a corrupt "backup" is worse than none.

Mains and lithium can hurt yousafety

Lots of cheap gadgets use a non isolated supply where the low voltage "ground" is tied straight to the AC line, so clipping an earthed probe to it can make a lethal fault. The pro move is to just not probe the mains side: pull the firmware off the low voltage logic with the AC section unpowered. If you truly must probe it live, use a 1:1 isolation transformer (it removes the earth path, it does not lower the voltage, mains is still lethal). Lithium cells vent and burn if you puncture, short, or overcharge them, so disconnect the battery and run off your PSU.

◆ numbers worth knowing
  • ESD damage on the touchiest parts: <250 V, way below what you can feel
  • Common rails: 1.8 / 3.3 / 5 V (also 1.2, 2.5). Measure, don't assume
  • Li-ion cell: 3.7 V nominal, 4.2 V max charge, ~2.5-3.0 V floor
  • Good backup = 2 reads, matching SHA-256, before any write
▲ don't
  • Bond your bare wrist straight to earth, or leave the strap on near live mains.
  • Trust a single flash read, or run the PSU with no current limit.
  • Desolder before photographing the board and labeling every wire. That photo is what saves you at 1am.
field manual When it goes wrong
  • Board browns out, or the PSU current limit trips the instant you probe. Cause: a short (solder bridge, backwards part), or you are probing a non-isolated mains supply. Play: set the current limit low and raise it slowly, so a short trips CC mode instead of cooking a trace; if voltage collapses, cut power and find the short. ⚠ never probe the mains side of a non-isolated board, its "ground" can sit at line potential.
02

Instruments: multimeter, logic analyzer & scope

You map a board with your hands before any iron or probe touches it. The multimeter finds ground and the rails, the logic analyzer decodes what's actually on a wire, and a scope shows you the analog truth when something's weird.

The multimeter, four modes and donebasics

Live in four settings: continuity (the beep) to find ground and trace nets, DC volts to read a rail against ground, and resistance and diode mode for parts (power off only, since they push their own current and a live rail gives garbage and can hurt the meter's input). Find ground first: continuity, black probe on obvious metal ground, and it beeps against any other ground. Then clip black there and let the red probe do all the walking.

Spot the rails, then the candidate signalsbasics

Power the board (USB or DC jack, never the mains side), DC volts, and sweep red across the header. A rail is a rock steady number (5V, 3.3V, or a 1.8V core). A UART TX pin idles at the logic voltage and then jitters for a second or two at power on while the banner prints, that boot time twitch is the reliable tell. A trick that saves headaches: read the idle TX voltage first, because that number is your logic level and it decides whether you need a level shifter.

The logic analyzer turns guesses into decodeslevel up

A $10 eight channel FX2 clone running sigrok/PulseView is the single best upgrade after the multimeter. Hook it to a mystery header at boot and the protocol decoders read UART, SPI, and I2C straight off the wire, so "is this UART or SPI?" stops being a guess. It also nails an unknown baud: capture TX, measure the narrowest pulse (that's one bit), and baud = 1/that. Sample at least four times faster than the signal you're chasing.

In-circuit resistance lies to yougotcha

Measure resistance on a soldered board and your part sits in parallel with every other path between those nodes, so a 10k pull up can read 300 ohms. Treat in-circuit resistance as "tied hard to a rail or not," never as a real value. For an actual value you'd lift one leg, but you rarely need to.

◆ quick specs
  • Black lead in COM, red in VΩ. Red left in the 10A jack while reading volts is a dead short.
  • Bit-width to baud: 8.68 µs ≈ 115200, 17.36 ≈ 57600, 104 ≈ 9600
  • Sample rate: ≥ 4× the fastest edge you want to see
▲ corrected
  • Never read resistance/continuity/diode on a powered board, those modes push their own current. Measure volts live, everything else dead.
  • RX often just floats, so don't lean on it sitting near Vcc. Find TX (the pin that bursts at boot) first.
  • Confirm the dial is on DC volts, not AC or a current range, before you touch a live rail.
field manual When it goes wrong
  • Not sure a pad is UART. Cause: a multimeter shows idle levels, not traffic. Play: put a logic analyzer on it and let sigrok decode: real UART turns into ASCII at some baud, and TX shows a dense burst at power-on. ⏱ confirms it before you solder anything
  • Resistance or continuity readings look nonsense on a live board. Cause: those modes push their own current, a powered rail corrupts them. Play: measure volts live, everything else with the board off. In-circuit resistance is only a hint ("tied hard to a rail or not"), never a real value.
03

Soldering, rework & chip-off

Nobody tells beginners the real secret: soldering is mostly flux and heat control, not a steady hand. Get those two right and you'll be tacking wires to 0.5mm test points and lifting chips in a weekend.

Flux is the whole gamebasics

Solder only flows onto clean, hot metal, and flux strips the oxide so it wets. If a joint fights you, add flux, don't crank the heat. A no clean flux pen plus a syringe of tacky gel is the most underrated gear on the bench. Heat the joint (chisel tip, both pad and lead) and feed solder into the joint so its own heat melts it, not onto the tip.

Read the joint, but know your alloyjudgment

A good joint is a smooth concave fillet that climbs the lead. On leaded (Sn63/Pb37) it's also shiny, but a perfect lead free (SAC305) joint looks matte and grainy, so judge by fillet shape and wetting, not shine. A dull ball sitting on top is cold (reheat it); solder spanning two pads is a bridge (wick it away).

Chip-off with hot airlevel up

When a chip won't read in place, take it off. Flood it with flux, sweep the nozzle until every leg is molten, and the part lifts free. Tape neighbors with Kapton, and preheat big ground plane boards so you don't warp the PCB or crack BGA balls. This is your fallback for a noisy SPI chip and the main event for eMMC.

In-circuit first, chip-off when it fights backstrategy

Always try reading a chip in place first, holding the SoC in reset so it doesn't fight you for the bus (more on that in the SPI and eMMC sections). Two identical back to back reads means you're done. Only reach for hot air when the board simply won't give you a clean, repeatable dump.

◆ temperatures
  • Leaded Sn63/Pb37 melts 183 °C, iron ~315-345 °C
  • Lead-free SAC305 melts ~217 °C, iron ~350-380 °C
  • Hot-air removal: leaded ~300-350, lead-free ~350-400 °C (use the lowest that reflows)
▲ watch out
  • Pull the lithium battery before hot air, and ventilate: flux fumes and leaded solder are toxic, wash your hands.
  • Don't run the iron cool "to be safe." Long contact does more damage than a hot tip touched briefly.
  • Over-temp on a BGA cracks the die or lifts pads, and there's no undo.
field manual When it goes wrong
  • Solder won't take, the joint fights you. Cause: not enough flux, or a tip with too little thermal mass. Play: add flux (don't crank the temperature), use a chisel tip, heat both pad and lead, feed solder into the joint. On lead-free a good joint looks matte, so judge by fillet shape, not shine.
  • BGA warps or cracks during chip-off. Cause: a big ground plane sinks heat, so you overheat one spot. Play: preheat the board, Kapton the neighbors, use the lowest air temp that actually reflows.
04

UART: the fastest way in

A serial console is the single highest yield foothold in hardware hacking. Four pads that a lot of consumer gear leaves wide open, often dropping you straight to the boot log, the bootloader, or an unauthenticated root shell. The advanced work isn't finding the port, it's reading its level and baud off the wire and knowing what to do once you're in.

Find the pads, then sniff safelybasics

Look for a row of 3-4 pads or an unpopulated header near the SoC. Multimeter finds GND (0Ω to ground) and the rails; TX is the one that idles high and jitters at boot. To just listen, wire only your adapter's RX to the target's TX plus a common ground and leave your TX disconnected, zero risk of injecting anything. To interact, cross them: adapter TX to device RX, RX to TX, share ground, and never wire VCC. Framing is almost always 8N1.

Match the level or fry a pinsafety

3.3V is most common, but plenty of modern SoCs run the console at 1.8V, and 5V is rare. There's no telling by looking, so measure. A 3.3V adapter into a 1.8V input can wreck it, so drop in a level shifter (a BSS138 board is fine to ~1MHz, or use a Tigard that sets its own IO voltage). And never wire a real RS-232 port (±12V) to a TTL UART, that kills the chip instantly.

Interrupt U-Boot and own the environmentthe good part

During boot U-Boot prints "Hit any key to stop autoboot" and counts down. Spam a key and you drop to the => prompt. Now printenv dumps every variable (bootargs, bootcmd, server IPs, and frequently leaked WiFi keys), setenv edits in RAM, and you skip saveenv to keep changes volatile and reversible. There are also raw memory primitives (md, mw, cp) that can read secrets from RAM or even dump firmware.

Force a shell with init=/bin/shthe good part

Classic escalation: append init=/bin/sh to the kernel command line so the kernel execs a shell before any auth runs, dropping you to uid 0. Reuse the existing bootargs and just add the token, then boot. Expect a read only rootfs (mount -o remount,rw /), no networking or services, and a watchdog that may reboot you, so disable it. If the prompt is locked with bootdelay=-2, this is where you pivot to a flash-line glitch (see the glitching section).

open the console, sweep the baud
ls /dev/ttyUSB* /dev/ttyACM*          # which device did the adapter become?
picocom -b 115200 /dev/ttyUSB0        # exit: Ctrl-A Ctrl-X
# garbage? sweep bauds with a plain reader (stty + cat), not picocom in a loop:
for b in 115200 57600 38400 19200 9600 74880; do
  echo "== $b =="; stty -F /dev/ttyUSB0 $b raw -echo; timeout 3 cat /dev/ttyUSB0
done
# in U-Boot: dump the env, force a shell, keep it volatile (no saveenv)
=> printenv
=> setenv bootargs "${bootargs} init=/bin/sh" ; boot

field manual When it goes wrong
  • UART spits garbage. Cause: wrong baud, wrong logic level, inverted signal, or it isn't UART. Play: sweep the standard bauds (115200 57600 38400 19200 9600 74880); if a level looks off, measure TX at idle; if nothing decodes, confirm it's really UART with a logic analyzer + sigrok.
  • UART is totally silent. Cause: you are on RX (it floats), TX/RX are swapped, or the console is disabled in bootargs. Play: find the pad that bursts at power-on, that's TX; swap TX/RX; read the boot log's console= to see the intended port. ⚠ a TX-only console lets you read but not type, don't assume it's "locked".
  • U-Boot won't stop at autoboot. Cause: bootdelay is 0/-1/-2, or you're mashing keys too late. Play: spam a key from the instant of power-on. If it's genuinely locked (bootdelay=-2), pivot to glitching the flash data line to force a recovery fallback, not the UART TX line.
  • Root shell, but the rootfs is read-only with no tools. Cause: SquashFS is read-only, and init=/bin/sh brought up no services. Play: mount -o remount,rw /, use BusyBox applets, and exfil over the same console (tar+base64) or via tftp/nc if the network is up. ⚠ disable the watchdog first, or it reboots you mid-work.
05

JTAG, SWD & boundary scan

JTAG and its two wire ARM cousin SWD are the debug backbone of almost every board. If the vendor left them live you get to halt the CPU, read memory, and dump flash with zero desoldering. This goes past "find four pins and run OpenOCD" into boundary scan tricks and what to do when the port is fused off.

The signals, and SWD reusing thembasics

JTAG has four mandatory wires: TCK (clock), TMS (state), TDI (in), TDO (out), plus optional resets. SWD collapses it to SWDIO + SWCLK on ARM Cortex, and it literally reuses the JTAG header pins (SWDIO on TMS, SWCLK on TCK). If only two debug lines respond, assume SWD. Heads up though, plenty of application SoCs route only JTAG, so don't count on SWD being there.

Find an unlabeled portrecon

On a stripped board the debug pads are unmarked. JTAGenum (firmware for an Arduino/Pico) permutes a bunch of GPIOs watching for a valid IDCODE; Joe Grand's JTAGulator is purpose built hardware with 24 protected channels and adjustable target voltage (1.2 to 3.3V) that runs IDCODE and BYPASS scans. Both drive unknown pins through every permutation, so treat results as candidates and tie grounds together first.

Boundary scan: read a flash through the CPU's pinsdeep

Every compliant pin has a scan cell between the pad and the core. SAMPLE snapshots pin states (safe); EXTEST disconnects the core and drives the pins straight from that register. With the chip's BSDL file you know which register bit maps to which ball, so you can bit-bang the CPU's own GPIOs that wire to an external SPI/NAND flash and clock the flash out with no firmware running. It's painfully slow but it works when you can't desolder, and boundary scan often survives "debug disable" fuses because it's a separate test feature.

gdb over the DAP, and when it's fuseddeep

OpenOCD speaks CoreSight and gives you a gdb server on 3333, telnet on 4444. Halt the core, dump_image fw.bin 0x08000000 0x100000, done. But if IDCODE reads fine while memory comes back all zeros or faults, the part is read protected: STM32 RDP, Nordic APPROTECT, eFuses. Bypasses are almost always fault injection, not software (see glitching). On MIPS routers you'll meet EJTAG instead, where PrAcc mode makes the CPU service memory accesses for you, which is how bricked WRT54G style routers get de-bricked.

EJTAG and PrAcc, for realMIPS

On MIPS routers the debug port isn't ARM's DAP, it's EJTAG, and the lever is PrAcc (processor access). Halt the core and it jumps into dmseg, the debug segment at 0xFF20_0000, running from the debug exception vector at 0xFF20_0200, where every fetch, load, and store becomes a transaction the probe services by hand. The handshake lives in the EJTAG control register: when PrAcc=1 the CPU is stalled waiting; you read the Address register to see what it wants, check PRnW for read vs write, put the value in the Data register, then clear PrAcc to let it run. That's how you spoon-feed a tiny flash-writer into a bricked WRT54G with no working bootloader; the classic HairyDairyMaid/TJTAG debrick utilities are exactly this loop, and OpenOCD drives it through a mips_m4k target.

06

SPI-NOR & flashrom

SPI-NOR is where most small devices keep firmware, and it's the single easiest chip to dump. Find the little 8 pin flash near the SoC, clip on, and read the whole image in seconds. The intermediate stuff is winning the fight for the bus and not frying a 1.8V part.

One pinout to rule them allbasics

Every 25-series 8 pin part (Winbond W25Q, Macronix MX25, GigaDevice GD25) shares the JEDEC layout in the diagram. Orient by the pin-1 dot, a clip flipped 180° swaps power and ground and kills the chip. On a plain single SPI hookup, tie /WP (pin 3) and /HOLD (pin 7) high or the chip stays held. And the digits are megabits: W25Q128 is 16 MB, not 128.

Win the bus: hold the SoC off itthe trick

In-circuit reads come back as garbage (all 0xFF, or different every time) because the SoC drives the same lines. Two fixes: pull the SoC's /RST to ground so its SPI pins tri-state, or lift the flash's VCC (pin 8) and power the chip only from your programmer. Start the SPI clock low (512 kHz to 8 MHz through a clip), 33 MHz is usually too fast on long wires.

The 1.8V trap and the CH341A hazardsafety

Decode the suffix before you wire anything: Winbond JV/FV = 3.3V, JW/FW = 1.8V (roughly 1.7 to 1.95V); Macronix MX25L = 3.3V, MX25U = 1.8V. Hitting a 1.8V part with 3.3V destroys it. Separately, the common black CH341A drives its data lines at ~5V even at "3.3V", so use a properly modded board or a Tigard, and for a 1.8V part a CH341A is out entirely (no mod makes it 1.8V), you need a level-selectable tool.

Write protection, if you ever write backdeep

Reads ignore write protection, but any erase or program silently fails until you clear it. It lives in the status registers (the BP/TB/SEC/CMP bits plus SRP0/SRP1). flashrom --wp-status shows it, --wp-disable clears it. The gotcha: if the hardware /WP pin is held low with SRP0 set, the register is frozen and software can't unlock it, so tie /WP high first. Some parts even ship with BP bits set by default.

flashrom: detect, read, verify
flashrom -p ch341a_spi                        # identify part + voltage FIRST (no -r/-w)
flashrom -p ch341a_spi -r dump1.bin
flashrom -p ch341a_spi -r dump2.bin
sha256sum dump1.bin dump2.bin && cmp dump1.bin dump2.bin   # two reads must match
binwalk dump1.bin                             # sanity: U-Boot + kernel + SquashFS = clean read
# Raspberry Pi as SPI host, drop the clock if unstable:
flashrom -p linux_spi:dev=/dev/spidev0.0,spispeed=4000 -r dump.bin

field manual When it goes wrong
  • SPI read is all 0xFF/0x00, or differs between dumps. Cause: the SoC still drives the bus / CS not low / chip unpowered / clip on backwards. Play: tie the SoC /RST to GND to tri-state its pins ⏱ saves a desolder; if not, lift the flash VCC (pin 8) and power it from the programmer only; drop the clock to 512kHz-4MHz on long wires; re-seat the clip with flux. ⚠ a clip flipped 180° swaps VCC/GND and KILLS the chip, orient by the pin-1 dot BEFORE powering.
  • flashrom says "no flash device found". Cause: VCC/GND orientation, a CH341A driving ~5V on the data lines, or an unknown chip ID. Play: re-check pin-1 orientation and voltage; if the JEDEC ID is ambiguous, force the exact part with -c "<chip>". Confirm your programmer's real I/O voltage with a meter.
  • Detects the chip, but erase/write fails silently or verifies the old value. Cause: status-register write-protect (BP/SRP bits), or the hardware /WP pin held low. eMMC & raw NAND

When a device is too big for an 8 pin SPI chip (phones, tablets, routers, cameras), firmware lives in eMMC or raw NAND. eMMC is the friendly one because a controller hides the messy physics. Raw NAND hands you the naked array and makes you do the forensic math yourself.

eMMC is basically an SD card with a jobbasics

It's raw NAND plus a controller in one BGA, speaking the JEDEC MMC protocol, electrically the same family as an SD card. The controller does wear leveling, ECC, and bad blocks, so you read clean logical sectors. And it always boots in 1-bit mode, so you only need six lines to get a full dump: CLK, CMD, DAT0, VCC, VCCQ, and ground.

The eMMC-to-SD reader hackthe trick

Because it's MMC, solder those points to a cheap USB microSD reader and the host enumerates it as /dev/sdX, then it's just dd. Two things bite people: VCCQ has to be happy at the reader's 3.3V (many modern parts run 1.8V IO, so measure, wrong VCCQ can damage the die), and you must hold the SoC in reset (not the eMMC's own RST_n) so the CPU isn't booting off the same chip and fighting you.

Don't miss the hidden partitionsloot

The disk you enumerate is only the user area. eMMC also has boot0/boot1 (where the bootloader and fuse data often live), RPMB (authenticated anti-rollback, you can't read it without the key), and up to four GPPs. On Linux they show up as mmcblk0boot0 etc, exported read only until echo 0 > .../force_ro. mmc extcsd read dumps the config register. A naive "dd the user area" misses the good stuff.

Raw NAND: a dump is not a filesystemdeep

Raw NAND (TSOP-48) gives you pages plus OOB/spare bytes holding ECC and bad-block markers. Three transforms stand between the dump and a mountable image: correct the bit errors with the exact ECC the SoC's controller used (Hamming or BCH, and it's the controller that defines the scheme, not the chip), skip the factory bad blocks, and often reverse a per-page scrambler that makes the raw silicon look like pure noise. Only then does binwalk see anything. Tools like OFRAK and Joshua Wise's FTL recovery show the full pipeline.

The shortcut: read through the controllerescape hatch

Before you reverse ECC and a scrambler by hand, try the easy path: read the NAND through the SoC's own controller while the device runs. With a shell, nanddump -f fw.bin -o /dev/mtd0 pulls a clean image, the driver already applied ECC and descrambling and the -o omits the OOB. That sidesteps the whole forensic pipeline. You only fall back to the offline reconstruction when the chip is dead or desoldered: then you strip the OOB yourself, and to spot a scrambler, look at a region that should be erased, if the 0xFF pages come back as high-entropy noise instead of flat 0xFF, a per-page randomizer is on and you need the controller's PRNG to undo it.

I2C, SPI sniffing & serial EEPROMs

new

Flash isn't the only place secrets live. A tiny 8 pin I2C EEPROM next to the SoC often holds config, calibration, MAC addresses, and sometimes credentials or keys. Two wires, cheap tools, quick win.

I2C in one paragraphbasics

Two wires, SDA (data) and SCL (clock), shared by a master and several devices, each at its own 7 bit address, with pull up resistors (~4.7k) to Vcc. Tap SDA/SCL with a logic analyzer and the sigrok I2C decoder to sniff config traffic passively, or become the master with a Bus Pirate or a Raspberry Pi and read a chip directly.

Dumping a 24-series EEPROMthe win

These live at 0x50 to 0x57. On Linux with a USB-I2C adapter, i2cdetect finds it and i2cdump reads it. The classic trap: on small parts (24C04/08/16) the "address" pins double as internal page selectors, so a 24C08 answers on several addresses and you must read each page. From 24C32 up (32 kbit / 4KB and larger) the chip switches to a 2 byte word address instead. Get that wrong and you get a truncated or repeating dump.

Sniff SPI instead of clipping italt

Sometimes you don't want to fight the SoC for a flash chip, you just want to watch it boot. Put the logic analyzer on CLK/MOSI/MISO/CS and the sigrok SPI decoder reconstructs exactly what the SoC reads at power on, including the early bootloader fetch. It's passive, non destructive, and great for understanding a boot flow before you commit to a dump.

# 24C32 and up use a 2-byte word address; use eeprom/at24 kernel driver or a programmer

Fault injection (glitching)

This is the door for chips that just say no: read protected flash, encrypted firmware, secure boot. Instead of finding a software bug, you shove the chip outside its safe envelope for a few nanoseconds so it miscomputes one instruction at exactly the right moment. That single corrupted instruction can skip a signature check or flip a "deny" into an "allow". People have done it to hardware wallets, game consoles, and a Starlink dish, often with under $100 of gear.

Break one instruction, not the chipidea

A CPU assumes stable voltage and a clean clock. Violate that for a moment and a transistor latches wrong, so one instruction executes incorrectly: a compare returns the wrong answer, a branch isn't taken. You don't need surgical control over what breaks, just that it lands on a security critical instruction. It's probabilistic, so the whole job is repeatable timing plus a parameter sweep plus a lot of attempts.

Voltage glitching with a crowbarcheapest

The classic rig is a "crowbar": a power MOSFET across Vcc that the glitcher briefly shorts to ground for tens to hundreds of nanoseconds. Colin O'Flynn's crowbar paper is the canonical reference and it's exactly what a ChipWhisperer builds in.

Clock, EM, and laserthe family

Clock glitch: insert one too short cycle to violate setup/hold, precise and cheap but only for externally clocked parts. EMFI: a coil pulse over the die, contactless, works through the package and on parts with internal regulators (O'Flynn's open source PicoEMP is the entry tool). Laser: a focused pulse through thinned silicon at ~1064nm gives single bit, single cycle precision, the most powerful and the most expensive.

Trigger, sweep, detectthe loop

A glitch is useless if you can't place it at the same instant every time, so triggering is half the battle: a reset edge, a GPIO or UART byte, or a power pattern (a SAD trigger fires when the live trace matches a stored fingerprint). Then sweep offset, width, and strength, classify every attempt (normal / reset / mute / crash / win), and plot it. The golden glitch almost always sits on the boundary of the reset region, so you scan coarse, find that frontier, and zoom in. Yields under 1% are normal, so automate and be patient.

The crowbar, for realcircuit

The crowbar is an N-channel power MOSFET: drain on the rail you want to collapse, source to GND. Drive the gate high and it saturates, shorting the rail to ground for tens to hundreds of ns, then releases. Two details make the rig. First, the gate is capacitive, so to switch in a few ns you need a gate driver pushing amps into it; a bare GPIO is too slow and the pulse turns into a lazy ramp (the ChipWhisperer already carries the driver and MOSFET on glitch_hp/glitch_lp). Second, the rail's decoupling caps are charge reservoirs that hold the voltage up and swallow your ns dip, so desolder the big ones near the chip to let the crowbar actually pull the rail down, leaving just enough that the board still boots between tries. And aim at the rail the core runs on: on an STM32F2/F4 that's VCAP (the internal 1.2V regulator's decoupling pin), not the 3.3V VDD.

Trigger and oracle firstprep

Before you sweep a single parameter, lock down two repeatable things. The trigger anchors t-zero; on a concrete target the most reliable is the reset edge: fire on the rising nRST and count ext_offset cycles to the target instruction (the RDP check, the signature compare). A UART/GPIO byte the target emits, or a SAD trigger matching a power pattern, work when there's no clean edge. The oracle is how you know you won without watching: flash that returns bytes != 0xFF, debug that answers after being blocked, a return value that only exists past the check. No oracle means you can't classify shots, and without classification the sweep is noise.

Don't copy numbers, characterizethe search

The winning (ext_offset, width) depends on this die, this layout, this supply, and even temperature, so copying a number from a writeup almost never reproduces. Sweep coarse first: a wide ext_offset at a big step to bracket the window, a few widths, and classify every shot as normal / reset / mute / crash / win. The reset region is your map: the win almost always sits on the boundary between "normal" and "reset", strong enough to corrupt one instruction, weak enough not to drop the chip. Find that edge, shrink the range around it, cut the step, and raise the attempts per point (it's probabilistic; 1 win in 500 only shows if you insist). Under 1% yield is normal, so automate and let it run; change the board or the temperature and re-characterize.

Side-channel analysis

new

Glitching makes a chip misbehave. Side-channel analysis just watches it behave and reads the secret out of the leakage. The current a chip draws depends on the data it's crunching, so with enough power traces and some statistics you can pull an AES key out of a device that has no software bug at all.

Why chips leakidea

In CMOS logic, a gate burns a little more power when a bit flips than when it stays put, so the instantaneous current carries a faint imprint of the data being processed. Measure that current over time and you get a power "trace." SPA reads secrets from a single trace by eye (an RSA square-and-multiply looks different for a 1 vs a 0). DPA and CPA use statistics over many traces to pull out a key even when the leak is buried in noise.

Measuring it: shunt or EM probesetup

Put a small shunt resistor in the chip's Vcc line and measure the voltage across it (current = data-dependent power), or hold a near-field EM probe over the die to pick it up contactlessly. Then trigger on the crypto op and capture. A ChipWhisperer bundles the shunt, a synchronized ADC, and the target, which is why it's the standard learning platform.

Recovering AES-128 with CPAthe attack

The trick is that AES's first-round SubBytes depends only on one key byte and one known plaintext byte, so you attack one byte at a time. For each of 256 guesses you predict the leakage (usually the Hamming weight of the SBox output) and correlate that prediction against your measured traces. The correct guess spikes in correlation and the other 255 stay flat. Repeat for all 16 bytes and you have the key. An unprotected software AES often falls in a few hundred to a few thousand traces.

Alignment, POIs, and countermeasuresdeep

Real traces are misaligned and noisy, so you preprocess: align (even elastic alignment via dynamic time warping), filter, and pick points of interest, and use TVLA (a fixed vs random t-test, |t| > 4.5) to check whether a device leaks at all before you attack. Template attacks profile a clone device first and are the strongest class when you can. Defenses are masking (randomize intermediate values), hiding (shuffling, random delays), and constant time code, which raise the trace count rather than making it impossible.

The measurement rigsetup

Two ways to see the current. A shunt: a small resistor (~1-50 Ω) in series with the chip's Vcc or ground, and you probe the voltage across it. Bigger R means more signal but more IR drop, so it can brown the chip out; put it in the ground leg to keep the probe near GND. A near-field EM probe is contactless, a tiny loop over the die or a decoupling cap, no cut traces, and you can move it to the leakiest spot. Either way, trigger on the crypto op (a GPIO the target raises around encrypt, or a pattern trigger) and sample well above the device clock, roughly 5-10×, so each clock cycle spreads over several points.

TVLA first: does it even leak?go / no-go

Before you spend hours on CPA, run TVLA as a go/no-go. Capture two trace sets, one with a fixed plaintext and one with random plaintext (keep the key fixed), then run Welch's t-test point by point. If |t| crosses 4.5 anywhere in the crypto window, the device leaks and an attack is worth it; if it stays flat, either it's well protected or your rig/trigger is wrong, fix that before attacking. TVLA also tells you where in time the leak is, which seeds your points of interest and your alignment. It's the cheapest hour you'll spend.

Acquire, unpack & the automated pipeline

A firmware image is a container of containers: a vendor header, compressed blobs, and one or more filesystems concatenated with padding and checksums. The unpacker's job is to find each chunk's start and end, carve it, decompress or mount it, and recurse. Getting the end offset right is what separates the modern tools from the old ones.

binwalk v3 and unblobbasics

binwalk got a full Rust rewrite (v3.1.0, ReFirmLabs, Oct 2024): same binwalk -e / -M (matryoshka recursion) muscle memory, faster, plus a library API. unblob (ONEKEY) is the accuracy play: 78+ formats, it computes each chunk's end from the format spec so it doesn't over-extract into the false-positive trees binwalk is infamous for, runs sandboxed and unprivileged, and even patched path-traversal bugs in the extractors it drives. Reach for unblob when binwalk's output is a mess.

Front-load the boring part: SBOM then CVEspipeline

Modern review is a straight line you can script: extract, build a Software Bill of Materials, map it to known CVEs, then reverse only what's left. syft inventories the rootfs into CycloneDX/SPDX; cve-bin-tool (Intel, ~448 checkers) fingerprints component versions inside stripped binaries (OpenSSL, BusyBox, zlib, expat) and queries NVD/OSV/others. The n-day pass runs itself so your time goes to the parts a scanner can't reach.

EMBA, FACT, cwe_checkerautomated

EMBA (Michael Messner / Siemens Energy) orchestrates the whole OWASP-FSTM flow: extract, static analysis, SBOM, CVE aggregation, system emulation, and a browsable HTML report. It wires in cwe_checker (Fraunhofer FKIE), which lifts ARM/MIPS/PPC/x86 binaries through Ghidra P-Code and flags ~18 CWE classes (CWE-78 command injection, CWE-119/125/787 overflows, CWE-134 format string). FACT adds a web UI whose signature feature is diffing two firmware versions to spot silent patches.

Encrypted image? Entropy tells you to stopgem

Both tools compute entropy. A structured region around 0.9 to 0.99 is usually compression; near-uniform ~1.0 across the whole file with no low-entropy headers and nothing extractable is an encrypted or signed OTA blob. unblob adds a chi-square check to cut the guesswork. When you hit that wall, the software unpacker did its job by telling you extraction alone won't work: pivot to UART/JTAG or bootloader analysis to recover the key or a plaintext update.

Emulation, rehosting & fuzzing

modern

Rehosting means running firmware without its hardware so you can instrument it, snapshot it, and fuzz it in parallel. The whole field is a fight between fidelity and speed, and it split into two lines: Linux router/IoT rehosting, and bare-metal MCU rehosting where every peripheral register has to be modeled.

User-mode vs full-system QEMUbasics

qemu-user runs one Linux ELF and translates its syscalls to your host: fast, but no real filesystem, NVRAM, or sibling processes. qemu-system boots a whole kernel and rootfs, giving you the real environment (nvram, the web server plus its backend) at the cost of speed and a working kernel/DTB. Rule of thumb: user-mode a single reachable binary when the bug lives inside it, go full-system when the vulnerable code depends on device state set up elsewhere.

FirmAE and Greenhouse for Linux gearrouter/IoT

Firmadyne (2016) could only boot about 16% of images. FirmAE (ACSAC 2020) adds arbitration heuristics (patching boot, network, NVRAM, kernel) and pushes that to ~79% over 1,124 images, and found 12 0-days across 23 devices. Full-system is heavy though, so Greenhouse (USENIX Security 2023) rehosts just one service in user space, auto-rewriting the binary to stub out nvram_get, missing files, and ioctls, then wires it to AFL++. For a bug in one web binary, that's usually all you need.

The MCU peripheral problembare-metal

On Cortex-M there's no OS: firmware pokes memory-mapped I/O directly, so a QEMU that returns 0 for a UART status register leaves it busy-waiting forever. Three answers: HALucinator (2020) matches vendor HAL functions and swaps in Python handlers so the hardware "disappears"; P2IM infers register roles from access patterns; and Fuzzware (USENIX 2022) treats each unknown MMIO read as fuzz input and uses local symbolic execution to model which bits actually matter, so the fuzzer stops wasting energy on don't-care bits.

Then bolt on a fuzzerthroughput

Once it runs, point AFL++ at it (qemu_mode, unicorn_mode, CmpLog, persistent mode). FirmAFL pioneered the full-system-plus-user hybrid for Linux IoT. And the throughput race is real: SAFIREFUZZ (USENIX 2023) runs Cortex-M firmware natively on an ARM host via high-level emulation and binary rewriting, reporting over 600x the throughput of HALucinator. On the exotic end, FirmWire (NDSS 2022) full-system-emulates Samsung and MediaTek baseband and fuzzes the cellular stack, a target that used to be basically un-fuzzable.

It boots, but the web server won'thalf-boot

The common half-win: FirmAE boots the kernel and userland but the web UI never answers. Debug it in ./scratch/<iid>/, where FirmAE drops the serial log and its network guess: read the serial log for how far init got, and ping the IP it prints. If it's up but port 80 is dead, the httpd started before its config existed, common when nvram_get returns nothing or a mount is missing. Re-run in debug mode (./run.sh -d) to get a console, then inside check ps, netstat -tlnp, and start the httpd by hand to watch it fail. Half the time it's one missing nvram default or a hardcoded path to a partition that didn't mount.

Reverse engineering with radare2

radare2 shines on firmware because it assumes nothing: it'll open a raw headerless MIPS/ARM blob, take the arch and base address on the command line, analyze it, and emulate one function with ESIL, all without the hardware. The durable workflow is sink-to-source, and the modern r2 (5.9.x through 6.x) pulls in real decompilers on demand.

Load a raw blob correctlybasics

The single most important firmware skill. After a carve, tell r2 everything: r2 -a mips -b 32 -e cfg.bigendian=true -m 0x80000000 boot.bin. Set arch, word size, endianness (big-endian MIPS is the classic router gotcha), and the map/base address so absolute pointers resolve. Guess the arch wrong and analysis is garbage, so sanity-check that strings, prologues, and xrefs line up. Install with the git sys/install.sh, not distro packages (they lag).

Analysis passes and the five leversnavigation

r2 doesn't over-analyze by default. aaa is the usual pass (or run aac/aae/aar individually). Then afl~name lists functions, axt sym.imp.system shows who calls a sink (the backbone of sink-to-source), pdf @ sym.foo disassembles one, izz~keyword finds strings (use izz, not iz, on raw blobs), and VV opens the control-flow graph.

ESIL, r2pipe, and diffinglevel up

When a value is computed, ESIL emulates a function offline to resolve it (aei; aeim; aeip; aes, with e io.cache=true), which is how you unpack an obfuscated config string or model a checksum. r2pipe scripts the whole hunt from Python: every command returns JSON via cmdj(), so you list every caller of every sink across a whole extracted filesystem. And radiff2 -A -C old new diffs at the function level to reverse a patch or spot a backdoor between two firmware revisions.

Decompilers and the honest ecosystemtooling

Pull decompilers on demand: r2pm -Uci r2ghidra gives you pdg (Ghidra's actual decompiler as a plugin, great on MIPS/ARM), r2dec gives pdd. Around r2 sit Rizin and its Cutter GUI, Ghidra's headless analyzeHeadless to batch-decompile a whole tree, and Binary Ninja. New in 2024/2025: r2ai and decai add LLM-assisted decompilation and vuln-hinting inside r2. Treat that output as a hypothesis, not truth.

Automated bug hunting: taint & real CVEs

A vulnerability is a data-flow fact: attacker input (a source) reaches a dangerous operation (a sink) without sanitization. On embedded Linux that's an HTTP param or NVRAM value flowing into system() (command injection) or into strcpy() with no bounds (overflow). Bug hunting is finding that tainted path, and the modern tools automate exactly this.

Start at the border binarybasics

The process bound to the network (the httpd or a CGI handler) is where attacker input enters, so that's where you start. Grep the sinks and walk backward. Sinks: system/popen/execve and vendor wrappers like doSystem/CsteSystem (red flags, they wrap system() with device data), plus strcpy/strcat/sprintf/memcpy. Sources: websGetVar, recv, and config reads nvram_get/nvram_safe_get. The question is always: does an argument trace back to a source with no sanitizer in between?

SaTC: shared keywords bridge front-end to binarytaint

SaTC (USENIX Security 2021) noticed the same input name shows up in both the web front-end (an HTML/JS string like "ping_addr") and the back-end binary as a hardcoded string used to fetch that param. It pulls those shared keywords from /www, finds their references in the border binary with Ghidra headless, and runs directed taint (ref2sink_cmdi, ref2sink_bof). That found 33 previously-unknown bugs, mostly command injection, including the D-Link DIR-878.

KARONTE and EmTaint: the hard casestaint

Real firmware splits a request across processes: httpd writes a value into NVRAM, a second binary reads it and calls system(). Single-binary taint misses that. KARONTE (IEEE S&P 2020) models the multi-binary data flow across NVRAM/env/file/socket IPC. EmTaint (ISSTA 2023) tackles the aliasing problem on stripped MIPS/ARM with on-demand alias analysis, fast enough to run whole images. Ahead of any of these, cwe_checker is a cheap first pass, and angr proves reachability and solves for the triggering bytes.

From a hit to a confirmed findingtriage

A static path is a hypothesis. Confirm four things: is the handler reachable pre-authentication (those are the valuable ones)? does the code strip ;|&`$() or use an allowlist between source and sink? can you reproduce it by rehosting the border binary and sending the request? and does a version diff show the vendor silently patched it? Only a path that's reachable, unsanitized, and controllable is a real bug.

Secure boot, bootkits & supply chain

modern

Secure boot is a chain of cryptographic gates, each stage verifying the next, rooted in a key fused or provisioned into the platform. The 2022 to 2025 run of research shattered the idea that this chain is unbreakable in practice. This is background for defenders and researchers, not a recipe for attacking gear you don't own.

The UEFI chain: PK, KEK, db, dbxbasics

UEFI Secure Boot is a signature policy in NVRAM. The Platform Key (PK) is the root that authorizes changes to the Key Exchange Keys (KEK), which authorize updates to db (allowed signers) and dbx (the revocation list). Firmware only runs a bootloader whose signature chains to db and isn't in dbx. On Linux, distros ship shim (signed by Microsoft's CA) which then trusts the distro cert plus your Machine Owner Keys (MOK). Audit MOK with mokutil --list-enrolled, because that's exactly what bootkits abuse.

Secure boot vs measured bootenforce vs attest

Secure boot enforces: a bad signature halts the boot. Measured boot records: each stage hashes the next into a TPM PCR plus an event log, then a remote verifier decides via attestation. Measured boot doesn't stop a bootkit, it makes the change detectable after the fact, and sealing disk keys to PCRs (LUKS+TPM, BitLocker) ties data access to an unmodified chain. Read PCRs with tpm2_pcrread.

How the chain actually broke, 2023 to 2025bypass classes

BlackLotus (2023) was the first in-the-wild UEFI Secure Boot bypass, using "baton drop" (CVE-2022-21894) to strip the in-memory policy and bringing its own signed-but-vulnerable binaries. Bootkitty (Nov 2024) was the first UEFI bootkit aimed at Linux (a research PoC). LogoFAIL (2023) turned the boot-logo image parser into code execution during DXE, no signed executable touched. The pattern: an unsigned data file, a leaked binary, or a parser bug beats the crypto.

When the root of trust is fakesupply chain

PKfail (Binarly, 2024) found that on 800+ device models the Platform Key was an AMI test key whose certificate literally says "DO NOT TRUST", with some private halves leaked publicly, so anyone could sign a trusted update. The 2023 MSI breach similarly leaked Intel Boot Guard OEM keys. Crypto boot security is only as good as key management across a long OEM/ODM chain. On embedded ARM the same ideas appear as U-Boot FIT verified boot, Android AVB, and TF-A's TBBR anchored to a ROTPK hash in fuses, and they fail the same way when a key leaks, fuses aren't blown in production, or rollback protection is missing.

Dynamic analysis, methodology & disclosure

Everything past static filesystem review is dynamic: driving a live or emulated device, intercepting its management plane, and getting a debugger onto it. Run it against a methodology so it's repeatable, and finish with a report the vendor can actually act on.

OWASP FSTM as a checklist you runbasics

The Firmware Security Testing Methodology is nine ordered stages: recon, obtain firmware, analyze, extract the filesystem, analyze contents, emulate, dynamic analysis, runtime analysis, exploitation. Stages 1 to 5 are static; the point of 6 to 9 is that they force you off the disk onto a running target. Treat each stage as a gate with a concrete artifact (a booting emulator, an authenticated web session, a debugger attach), not a vibe.

Intercept the management planewhere the bugs are

Proxy the web UI through Burp or mitmproxy and map every hidden CGI. On OpenWrt-derived gear the config plane is ubus (JSON-RPC) and uci: from a shell try ubus list, ubus call system board, and check ACLs in /usr/share/rpcd/acl.d/. Over HTTP, uhttpd's ubus plugin enforces login and ACL, so weak session handling is the bug. For telemetry, subscribe to the broker with mosquitto_sub -t '#' and probe CoAP on UDP/5683.

The companion app is a shortcutmobile

The app is often the fastest way to understand a proprietary protocol and cloud API. Decompile the APK with jadx, pull hardcoded endpoints and keys, and use Frida to hook TLS and crypto at runtime, bypassing cert pinning and dumping the plaintext BLE/HTTP/MQTT the device actually trusts. Then replay that straight against the device or its API to test authorization boundaries.

Get a debugger on it, then fuzz the protocolruntime

With a shell, cross-compile a matching gdbserver, run gdbserver :1234 --attach <pid>, and connect with gdb-multiarch plus GEF or pwndbg. With no shell but exposed pads, bridge OpenOCD to gdb over JTAG. Once you know the protocol, fuzz it with boofuzz over TCP/UDP, paired with a liveness monitor so a hang counts as a fault. Match endianness exactly (mipsel vs mips).

Scale it, then disclose itthe deliverable

For a portfolio, wire EMBA (and its team front-end EMBArk) into CI so every build is scanned and diffed; commercial platforms like ONEKEY do continuous binary-SBOM and CVE matching. Then close the loop: coordinated disclosure (CISA CVD, ISO 29147/30111), a CVE from the right CNA, and a report with the exact affected version and hash, the file and function, the CWE, a reproducible PoC, a CVSS vector, preconditions, impact, and remediation. Attach the SBOM and your emulation steps so the vendor reproduces it without your rig.

◆ the evidence gate
  • Binary + function + address. Point at it.
  • The reachable path, source to sink, pre-auth or not.
  • The defect instruction, quoted from the disassembly.
  • Attacker position + impact + a reproducible PoC.
◆ two retest traps
  • Recompiled ≠ fixed. Hash the vulnerable material (the key, the RSA n/d), not the ELF.
  • Retest against production, not the beta. radiff2 the two and anchor the report where users are.
◆ work the methodology, not vibes

Three solid, reproducible findings beat twenty maybes, and a clean "checked, it's bounded" is worth writing down. Hunt like a second you is trying to kill every finding: the "secret" key turns out derivable, the "unbounded" copy is capped, the input isn't attacker controlled. Whatever survives that is real.
